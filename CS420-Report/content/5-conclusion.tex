\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether a highly accurate prediction necessarily comes with a reliable explanation. Within the scope of our experiments, the answer is negative. High accuracy reflects average performance over a test distribution and guarantees neither the stability, the uniqueness, nor the causal validity of a post-hoc explanation. Our random forest reaches about 97\% accuracy, yet for a correctly classified borderline instance LIME with 500 samples produced six different top-ranked features across 30 random seeds. LIME and SHAP agreed on confident instances but disagreed in ranking and sign on the borderline one, which is precisely the kind of case in which an explanation is most needed. Changing the SHAP background moved the base value of the random forest from 0.03 to 0.96 and reversed the sign of the leading attributions. Finally, removing redundant features more than doubled the attribution of \texttt{worst radius} without changing the accuracy, and an uninformative noisy copy of a feature received as much attribution as the original.

These findings suggest several practical guidelines. For tree ensembles, TreeSHAP is preferable, as it is exact, deterministic, and orders of magnitude faster than the model-agnostic estimators; the background set and the feature-perturbation mode should be reported with the explanation. In model-agnostic settings, KernelSHAP should be preferred when local accuracy matters. When LIME is used, explanations should be repeated over several seeds with a budget of at least several thousand samples, and the $R^2$ of the surrogate should be reported. In all cases, explanations should be validated with a deletion test against a random baseline, and statistical attribution should be clearly distinguished from causal effect when results are communicated to clinicians or other stakeholders.

Our study is limited to a single dataset and a single explained model. Future work could extend the analysis to further datasets and model families, compare against explainers that respect feature dependence, such as conditional or causal Shapley values, and develop standardized faithfulness benchmarks for medical applications.
