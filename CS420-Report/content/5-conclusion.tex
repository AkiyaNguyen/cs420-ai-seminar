\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether a highly accurate prediction necessarily comes with a reliable explanation. Within the scope of our experiments, the answer is negative: high accuracy reflects average performance over a test distribution and guarantees neither the stability, the uniqueness, nor the causal validity of a post-hoc explanation. Our random forest reaches about 97\% test accuracy, yet the explanations of a correctly classified borderline instance were unstable. With 500 samples, LIME produced six different top-ranked features across 30 random seeds, and even with 5000 samples the mean pairwise Jaccard@5 of its top-5 features was only 0.54. The explanations were also not unique. LIME and SHAP agreed on confidently classified instances but disagreed in ranking and sign on the borderline one, which is precisely the kind of case in which an explanation is most needed, and changing the SHAP background moved the base value $\phi_0$ from 0.03 to 0.96 and reversed the sign of the two leading attributions. Finally, the attributions did not reflect causal structure. Removing the perimeter and area features more than doubled the mean absolute TreeSHAP attribution of \texttt{worst radius}, while test accuracy decreased only from 97.37\% to 95.61\%; adding a redundant noisy copy of \texttt{worst radius} left accuracy unchanged and split the attribution almost equally between the original and the copy. On the positive side, all three explainers were considerably more faithful than a random baseline in the deletion test, with SHAP slightly ahead of LIME.

These findings suggest several practical guidelines. For tree ensembles, TreeSHAP is preferable: it computes exact Shapley values for the chosen background, is deterministic, and in our setting was several times faster than LIME and more than an order of magnitude faster than KernelSHAP. For any SHAP explanation, the background set, and for TreeSHAP also the feature-perturbation mode, should be reported with the explanation. In model-agnostic settings, KernelSHAP should be preferred when local accuracy matters, at a higher computational cost. When LIME is used, explanations should be repeated over several seeds with a budget of at least several thousand samples, and the $R^2$ of the surrogate should be reported. In all cases, explanations should be validated with a deletion test against a random baseline, bearing in mind that the test relies on a particular notion of feature absence, and statistical attribution should be clearly distinguished from causal effect when results are communicated to clinicians or other stakeholders.

Our study is limited to a single dataset, a single train/test split, and a single explained model. Future work could extend the analysis to further datasets and model families, examine how explanations vary across equally accurate models, compare against explainers that respect feature dependence, such as conditional~\cite{aas2021explaining} or causal~\cite{heskes2020causal} Shapley values, and develop standardized faithfulness benchmarks for medical applications.
